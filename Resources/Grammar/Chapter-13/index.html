Tenses — Part 2: Past and Future Forms

Study the past simple, past continuous, past perfect, past perfect continuous and common ways to refer to the future.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain the rules, recognise the target forms in context, avoid common errors and apply the patterns to new sentences.

  • Understand the core terms and distinctions.
  • Study examples in complete sentences, not as isolated rules.
  • Complete all 15 practice items before opening the answer key.

1. Past simple

Use the past simple for completed past actions, often with a definite time. Regular verbs usually take -ed; irregular verbs have individual forms. Questions and negatives use did + base verb.

We visited the museum yesterday. / Did she call? / He did not go.

2. Past continuous

Use was/were + verb-ing for an action in progress at a past time, background activity, or an action interrupted by another event.

I was cooking when the phone rang. / At eight, they were studying.

3. Past perfect and past perfect continuous

Past perfect (had + past participle) marks an event earlier than another past reference point. Past perfect continuous (had been + -ing) emphasises duration/activity before that point.

The train had left before we arrived. / She had been working for hours when the power failed.

4. Future meanings

Will + base verb can express predictions, spontaneous decisions and promises. Be going to often expresses prior plans or evidence-based predictions. Present continuous can express arranged future plans; present simple can describe schedules.

I’ll help you. / Look at those clouds—it’s going to rain. / We’re meeting at six.

5. Future continuous and future perfect

Will be + -ing describes an activity in progress at a future time. Will have + past participle describes something completed by a future deadline. Use time clauses with when, after and before carefully: usually present forms rather than will.

This time tomorrow, I’ll be flying. / By Friday, she will have finished. / Call me when you arrive.

Guided practice

Try each item first. Then open the answer key and read the explanation for any answer you missed.

  1. We ___ Dhaka last year. (visit)
  2. She ___ dinner when I arrived. (cook)
  3. The train ___ before we reached the station. (leave)
  4. They ___ for two hours before help came. (wait)
  5. I think it ___ tomorrow. (rain)
  6. Look at the sky! It ___. (rain)
  7. We ___ our cousins this evening. (meet)
  8. The bus ___ at 7:30 tomorrow. (leave)
  9. At 9 p.m. tonight, I ___ (study).
  10. By next month, he ___ the course. (complete)
  11. Correct: “Did you went there?”
  12. When she arrived, we ___ already ___. (eat)
  13. I ___ you when I get home. (call)
  14. Wait here until he ___. (come)
  15. At noon yesterday, they ___ a meeting. (have)
Show answer key and explanations
  1. visited — A completed action with last year takes past simple.
  2. was cooking — The action was in progress at the interruption.
  3. had left — Past perfect marks the earlier past event.
  4. had been waiting — Past perfect continuous emphasises duration before a past event.
  5. will rain — Will can express a prediction.
  6. is going to rain — Visible evidence supports going to.
  7. are meeting — Present continuous can describe an arrangement.
  8. leaves — Present simple is used for timetabled events.
  9. will be studying — Future continuous describes an activity in progress at a future time.
  10. will have completed — Future perfect marks completion by a deadline.
  11. Did you go there? — After did, use the base form.
  12. had; eaten — The meal happened before her arrival.
  13. will call — Will is natural for a future promise/decision.
  14. comes — Use present simple in a future time clause after until.
  15. were having — Past continuous describes an action in progress at a past time.

Revision checklist

  • I can explain the main rules in my own words.
  • I can recognise the correct form in a complete sentence.
  • I can explain why a common incorrect form is wrong.
  • I can apply the rule to a new example.

If an answer was wrong, identify the rule behind the mistake and try writing two original examples.
