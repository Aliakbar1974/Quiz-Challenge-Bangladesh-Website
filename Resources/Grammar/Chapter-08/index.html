GRAMMAR RESOURCE · CHAPTER 08

Adverbs: Meaning, Types and Position

Learn adverbs of manner, time, place, frequency, degree and viewpoint, their position in sentences, adjective–adverb differences and common errors.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain the main rules, identify the target forms in real sentences, avoid common errors and apply the rules to unfamiliar examples.

  • Understand the core terms and distinctions.
  • Study examples in context rather than memorising isolated rules.
  • Complete the practice before opening the answer key.

1. What is an adverb?

An adverb modifies a verb, an adjective, another adverb or sometimes a whole clause. It may tell us how, when, where, how often or to what degree something happens.

She spoke softly (modifies spoke). It is extremely cold (modifies adjective cold). He ran very quickly (very modifies quickly). Fortunately, nobody was hurt (comments on the whole clause).

Many adverbs end in -ly, but not all do; and some -ly words are adjectives, such as friendly and lovely.

2. Common types of adverbs

TypeQuestion answeredExamples
MannerHow?carefully, well, slowly
TimeWhen?today, soon, yesterday
PlaceWhere?here, outside, nearby
FrequencyHow often?always, often, rarely
DegreeTo what extent?very, almost, quite, too
Sentence / viewpointWhat is the speaker's stance?fortunately, honestly, probably

3. Position in a sentence

Adverb position depends on its type and emphasis. Manner adverbs often follow the verb or its object: “She answered politely.” Frequency adverbs usually come before a main verb but after be: “They often travel”; “She is always ready.” With auxiliaries, frequency adverbs commonly come after the first auxiliary: “He has never visited.”

Compare: “She nearly drove for six hours” (nearly modifies the amount of driving) and “She drove for nearly six hours” (nearly modifies the duration). Put an adverb near the word or idea it is intended to modify.

Time and place adverbials often occur at the end: “We met outside yesterday.” They can also be placed at the beginning for emphasis: “Yesterday, we met outside.”

4. Adjective or adverb?

Adjectives describe nouns or pronouns; adverbs often describe verbs or adjectives. After linking verbs such as feel, look, smell, sound and taste, an adjective often describes the subject.

  • She is a careful driver. / She drives carefully.
  • The soup tastes good. / The chef cooks well.
  • He works hard. (“Hardly” means scarcely, not “with great effort.”)
  • They arrived late. (“Lately” usually means recently.)

5. Degree, too and enough

Adverbs of degree include very, quite, rather, almost, nearly and extremely. Too often means more than is desirable; enough commonly follows an adjective or adverb but precedes a noun.

The tea is too hot to drink. / The tea is cool enough to drink. / We have enough time. / She did not speak loudly enough.

Avoid double negatives in standard exam English: “I hardly know anyone” is standard; “I don't hardly know nobody” is non-standard in most formal contexts.

Guided practice

Try each item first. Then open the answer key and read the explanation for any answer you missed.

  1. Choose: She sings (beautiful / beautifully).
  2. Choose: The flowers smell (sweet / sweetly).
  3. Where does often usually go: “I ___ read before bed”?
  4. Choose: He works (hard / hardly) every day.
  5. Choose: I have (never / never have) seen snow.
  6. Identify the adverb: “They arrived yesterday.”
  7. Choose: She is a (friendly / friendlily) person.
  8. Complete: The room is warm ___ to sleep in. (enough / too)
  9. What does “hardly” mean in “I hardly know him”?
  10. Choose: He did the work (good / well).
  11. Identify the degree adverb: “The test was extremely difficult.”
  12. Correct: “She speaks English fluent.”
  13. Choose: We met (outside yesterday / yesterday outside) as a neutral end position.
  14. Choose: The water is (too / enough) cold to swim in.
  15. What does “lately” usually mean?
Show answer key and explanations
  1. beautifully — The adverb modifies the verb sings.
  2. sweet — Sweet describes the flowers after the linking verb smell.
  3. I often read before bed. — Frequency adverbs usually precede a main verb.
  4. hard — Hard means with effort; hardly means scarcely.
  5. never — Never commonly follows the first auxiliary have.
  6. yesterday — It tells when they arrived.
  7. friendly — Friendly is an adjective describing person.
  8. enough — Enough follows the adjective warm.
  9. Almost not / scarcely — Hardly is not the adverbial form meaning with effort.
  10. well — Well is the adverb modifying did the work.
  11. extremely — It modifies the adjective difficult.
  12. She speaks English fluently. — The adverb fluently modifies speaks.
  13. outside yesterday — Place often precedes time at the end of a sentence.
  14. too — Too cold expresses an excessive degree for swimming.
  15. Recently — Lately differs in meaning from late.

Revision checklist

  • I can explain the main rule in my own words.
  • I can identify the target form in a sentence.
  • I can explain why a common incorrect example is wrong.
  • I can apply the rule in a new sentence.

If an answer was wrong, identify the rule behind the mistake and practise that pattern again.
