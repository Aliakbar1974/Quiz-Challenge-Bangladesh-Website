GRAMMAR RESOURCE · CHAPTER 40 · PART D

Grammar Mock Tests and Revision

Use timed mixed tests, an error log and targeted revision to prepare for competitive examinations and measure real progress.

Estimated study time: 12–18 minutes · 5 lesson sections · 15 practice questions with explanations

Your learning path

1. LearnRead the rules and examples carefully.
2. PractiseAnswer all 15 questions before revealing the key.
3. ReviewExplain each mistake and write a new example.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain the rules, recognise the target forms in context, avoid common errors and apply the patterns to new sentences.

  • Understand the core terms and distinctions.
  • Use examples to connect grammar rules with real sentences.
  • Complete all 15 practice items before opening the answer key.

1. Prepare a realistic test session

Choose a quiet period, set a fair time limit and answer without checking notes. Use a mix of topics and question types. Treat a practice score as feedback, not a measure of personal worth.

First attempt: answer independently; second stage: analyse mistakes.

2. Manage time strategically

Answer questions you can solve confidently first, mark uncertain items and return later. Read every option carefully, especially negatives such as not, unless and except.

Do not spend several minutes on one uncertain item while easy questions remain.

3. Review more than the score

Classify errors as knowledge gaps, misreading, rushed decisions or unfamiliar vocabulary. A wrong answer with a clear reason is more useful than a lucky guess.

Error log: question → my choice → correct answer → rule → new example.

4. Build a spaced revision plan

Review errors the same day, revisit them after a few days, and test again later. Mix weak topics with previously mastered ones to strengthen long-term recall.

Review after 1 day, 3 days and 7 days as a practical starting schedule.

5. Turn results into a next step

Use the pattern of errors to choose the next lesson. Revisit Chapters 31–39 as needed; repeat a mock only after reviewing the rules behind the previous mistakes.

Accuracy + explanation + later recall are stronger evidence of learning than a single score.

Guided practice

Try each item first without checking the answers. Afterward, open the key and read the explanation for every uncertain answer.

  1. In a mock test, what should you do first when you see a difficult question?
  2. You guessed correctly but cannot explain why. Should you count the rule as mastered?
  3. What should an error log include?
  4. Each of the questions ___ a clear explanation. (has / have)
  5. By the time the exam began, the candidates ___ their papers. (had received / have received)
  6. Choose the correct phrase: interested in (learn / learning)
  7. Correct: “Unless you don't hurry, you will miss the bus.”
  8. The committee ___ reached a decision. (has / have)
  9. Choose the correct connector: She was tired; ___, she finished the task. (nevertheless / because)
  10. Correct: “She explained me the answer.”
  11. Which is the correct first conditional?
  12. Choose the correct article: He is ___ European citizen. (a / an)
  13. What should you do after reviewing your mistakes?
  14. A student scores 80% but repeats the same errors. What is the best next step?
  15. What is the strongest evidence that you have learned a grammar rule?
Show answer key and explanations
  1. Skip or mark it temporarily and answer easier questions first if the test format allows. — Strategic time management protects time for questions you can answer confidently.
  2. No; review the rule and practise a new example. — A lucky guess does not demonstrate reliable understanding.
  3. The question, your answer, the correct answer, the rule and a new example. — This records the reasoning needed to avoid repeating the same mistake.
  4. has — Each is singular in standard agreement.
  5. had received — Past perfect expresses completion before a past reference point.
  6. learning — The preposition in is followed by a gerund.
  7. Unless you hurry, you will miss the bus. — Unless already means if not; an extra negative changes the intended logic.
  8. has — The committee is treated as one unit in this sentence; collective agreement can vary by meaning and dialect.
  9. nevertheless — Nevertheless expresses contrast between tiredness and completing the task.
  10. She explained the answer to me. — Explain normally takes the information as its direct object and uses to for the recipient.
  11. If she studies, she will pass. — Use a present form in the if-clause and will + base verb in the result clause.
  12. a — European begins with a /j/ (“you”) consonant sound.
  13. Write or answer a fresh question using each corrected rule, then revisit it later. — New examples and spaced review test whether learning transfers and lasts.
  14. Group the recurring errors by rule and revise those weak topics before another full test. — A targeted review addresses causes rather than merely repeating the same test.
  15. You can explain it, use it in a new sentence and recall it later. — Explanation, transfer and delayed recall provide stronger evidence than one score.

Learning reflection

  • I can explain the main rules in my own words.
  • I can recognise the correct form in a new sentence.
  • I can explain why a common incorrect form is wrong.
  • I can apply the rule without looking at the example.

Retention tip: Review your mistakes tomorrow, then again after three days and one week. Write one new example for each rule you missed.
