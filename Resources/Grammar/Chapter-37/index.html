GRAMMAR RESOURCE · CHAPTER 37 · PART D

Sentence Structure and Word Order

Improve sentence accuracy by analysing clause patterns, word order, parallelism, pronoun reference and information flow.

Estimated study time: 12–18 minutes · 5 lesson sections · 15 practice questions with explanations

Your learning path

1. LearnRead the rules and examples carefully.
2. PractiseAnswer all 15 questions before revealing the key.
3. ReviewExplain each mistake and write a new example.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain the rules, recognise the target forms in context, avoid common errors and apply the patterns to new sentences.

  • Understand the core terms and distinctions.
  • Use examples to connect grammar rules with real sentences.
  • Complete all 15 practice items before opening the answer key.

1. The core clause pattern

Many English clauses follow subject–verb–object or subject–linking verb–complement. Identify the main verb first, then determine what roles the other elements play.

The committee (subject) approved (verb) the proposal (object).

2. Adverbs and word order

Adverbs can appear in different positions, but placement affects emphasis and sometimes meaning. Frequency adverbs usually precede a main verb but follow be and the first auxiliary.

She often visits. / She is often late.

3. Questions and negative structures

Questions usually require auxiliary–subject inversion; when no auxiliary is present in present/past simple, use do/does/did. Keep the main verb in base form after do-support.

Where does she live? Not: Where does she lives?

4. Pronoun reference and parallelism

A pronoun should clearly refer to a specific noun. Items with the same function should have matching grammatical forms. Place modifiers near the words they describe.

Unclear: When Sara met Mina, she was late. Clear wording identifies who was late.

5. Avoid fragments and run-ons

A complete sentence normally needs an independent clause. Do not join independent clauses with a comma alone; do not leave a dependent clause standing alone unintentionally.

Fragment: Because the shop was closed. Complete: Because the shop was closed, we returned.

Guided practice

Try each item first without checking the answers. Afterward, open the key and read the explanation for every uncertain answer.

  1. Arrange into a standard sentence: “the proposal / approved / the committee.”
  2. Choose the correct word order: “She always is polite” or “She is always polite.”
  3. Correct: “Where she lives?”
  4. Correct: “Why did he left early?”
  5. Choose the parallel sentence: “He enjoys reading and to swim” or “He enjoys reading and swimming.”
  6. Fix the fragment: “Although the shop was closed.”
  7. Fix the comma splice: “The alarm rang, everyone left.”
  8. Choose the correct placement: “He nearly worked for six hours” if he worked for almost six hours.
  9. Correct: “She explained me the rule.”
  10. Complete: Neither the coach nor the players ___ ready. (was / were)
  11. Choose the correct question: “Does he know?” or “Does he knows?”
  12. Improve unclear reference: “When Nila called Rupa, she was driving.”
  13. Correct the modifier: “Covered in dust, Karim cleaned the shelf.”
  14. Choose: “I have never seen such a view” or “Never I have seen such a view.”
  15. What should you identify first when analysing a complex sentence?
Show answer key and explanations
  1. The committee approved the proposal. — The basic order is subject + verb + object.
  2. She is always polite. — Frequency adverbs normally follow the verb be.
  3. Where does she live? — Present-simple questions with a lexical verb use do-support and subject–verb inversion.
  4. Why did he leave early? — After did, use the base form leave.
  5. He enjoys reading and swimming. — The coordinated activities use matching gerund forms.
  6. Although the shop was closed, we returned home. — Although introduces a dependent clause that needs a main clause.
  7. The alarm rang, so everyone left. / The alarm rang; everyone left. — Two independent clauses need an appropriate connector or punctuation.
  8. He worked for nearly six hours. — Nearly should modify the duration six hours.
  9. She explained the rule to me. — Explain normally takes the thing explained before a to-phrase for the recipient.
  10. were — With nor, agreement commonly follows the nearer subject players.
  11. Does he know? — After does, the main verb uses its base form.
  12. Rewrite to name the driver: “Nila was driving when she called Rupa” or “Rupa was driving when Nila called.” — The original pronoun she has an unclear antecedent.
  13. Karim cleaned the shelf that was covered in dust. — As written, the modifier seems to describe Karim.
  14. I have never seen such a view. — Without emphatic fronting, the ordinary subject–auxiliary order is used.
  15. The main clause and its subject and verb. — Finding the clause skeleton helps you check word order, agreement and punctuation.
