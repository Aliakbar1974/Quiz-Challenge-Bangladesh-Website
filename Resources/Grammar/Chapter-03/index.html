GRAMMAR RESOURCE · CHAPTER 03

Nouns: Types, Countability and Possession

Learn common and proper nouns, concrete and abstract nouns, collective and compound nouns, countable and uncountable usage, possessives and noun functions with examples and practice.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain the main rules, identify the target forms in real sentences, avoid common errors and apply the rules to unfamiliar examples.

  • Understand the core terms and distinctions.
  • Study examples in context rather than memorising isolated rules.
  • Complete the practice before opening the answer key.

1. What is a noun?

A noun names a person, place, thing, substance, idea, quality or event. Nouns can function as subjects, objects, complements or objects of prepositions.

Subject: Honesty matters. Direct object: We admired the painting. Subject complement: Mina is a doctor. Object of a preposition: The keys are in the drawer.

To identify a noun, look at how a word functions in the sentence. A word ending in -tion or -ness is often a noun, but endings alone do not prove its function.

2. Common noun types

TypeMeaningExamples
CommonGeneral namecity, teacher, river
ProperSpecific name; usually capitalisedDhaka, Mr Rahman, the Padma
ConcreteSomething experienced through the sensesstone, music, perfume
AbstractIdea, quality, state or conceptfreedom, kindness, courage
CollectiveA group considered as a unitteam, audience, committee
CompoundTwo or more elements form one nountoothbrush, bus stop, mother-in-law
MaterialSubstance or material (traditional school category)gold, cotton, water

Categories can overlap. Gold is a material noun and usually uncountable when referring to the substance; a gold can occur in specialist contexts meaning a gold medal or award. Grammar depends on meaning and context.

3. Countable and uncountable nouns

Countable nouns have singular and plural forms and can follow numbers: one chair, three chairs. Uncountable nouns normally refer to substances, abstract concepts or mass categories and do not usually take a plural in that meaning: information, advice, furniture, rice.

CountableUsually uncountableUseful phrase
an idea / ideasinformationa piece of information
a suggestion / suggestionsadvicea piece of advice
a chair / chairsfurniturean item of furniture
a coin / coinsmoneya sum of money
Incorrect: She gave me many advices.
Correct: She gave me a lot of advice. / She gave me two pieces of advice.

Some nouns can be countable or uncountable with a change of meaning: chicken (meat) / a chicken (an animal); paper (material) / a paper (a document or academic article); experience (knowledge gained) / an experience (an event).

Abstract nouns are not automatically uncountable: we can say an idea, a fear, several difficulties. Choose the form according to the intended meaning.

4. Possessive nouns

  • Singular noun: add apostrophe + s — the student's book.
  • Regular plural ending in -s: add an apostrophe — the students' room.
  • Irregular plural not ending in -s: add apostrophe + s — children's clothes.
  • Joint ownership: usually mark the final name — Rafi and Mina's project.
  • Separate ownership: mark each name — Rafi's and Mina's projects.
Compare its (possessive: The cat licked its paw) with it's (it is / it has: It's raining). An apostrophe does not normally make a noun plural.

5. Nouns in context and common errors

A noun can act as a modifier before another noun: school bus, coffee cup, language teacher. The first noun is not necessarily plural even when the second noun is plural: shoe shops, not usually shoes shops.

  • Incorrect: The furnitures are expensive. Correct: The furniture is expensive.
  • Incorrect: Dhaka is a beautiful city. (No error in the noun forms; note proper-name capitalisation.)
  • Incorrect: The childrens are playing. Correct: The children are playing.
  • Incorrect: I need an information. Correct: I need some information / a piece of information.

Basic plural spelling rules are expanded in Chapter 11. For now, learn frequent irregular forms such as child/children, person/people, mouse/mice and foot/feet.

Guided practice

Try each item first. Then open the answer key and read the explanation for any answer you missed.

  1. Which is a proper noun: river, country, Bangladesh or student?
  2. In “Kindness earns respect,” what kind of noun is kindness?
  3. Choose the standard expression: many advice / much advice.
  4. Complete: Two pieces of ___ (information / informations).
  5. Which is normally a collective noun: committee, quickly or bright?
  6. Choose the correct possessive: the girls room (room belonging to several girls).
  7. Which is correct: childrens' toys / children's toys?
  8. In “She bought a paper,” is paper necessarily uncountable?
  9. Identify the noun used as a modifier: “a stone wall.”
  10. Choose the correct sentence: The furniture is old / The furniture are old.
  11. Which spelling is correct: mice / mouses (ordinary plural of mouse)?
  12. Choose: Rafi and Mina's house / Rafi's and Mina's house to show one jointly owned house.
  13. Can “an idea” be correct even though idea is abstract?
  14. In “The teacher praised the class,” what is the role of class?
  15. Correct the sentence: “She gave me two advices.”
Show answer key and explanations
  1. Bangladesh — It names a specific country and is capitalised.
  2. Abstract noun — It names a quality rather than a physical object.
  3. much advice — Advice is normally uncountable in standard English.
  4. information — The countable unit is pieces; information stays uncountable.
  5. committee — It names a group considered as a unit.
  6. the girls' room — A regular plural ending in -s takes an apostrophe after the s.
  7. children's toys — Children is an irregular plural not ending in s, so add 's.
  8. No; here it can mean a document or newspaper. — Countability can change with meaning.
  9. stone — Stone modifies the noun wall.
  10. The furniture is old. — Furniture is normally uncountable and takes singular agreement.
  11. mice — Mouse has the irregular plural mice.
  12. Rafi and Mina's house — The possessive marker usually follows the final name for joint ownership.
  13. Yes. — Abstract nouns may be countable when the meaning allows it.
  14. Direct object — It receives the action of praised.
  15. She gave me two pieces of advice. — Use a countable measure phrase with uncountable advice.

Revision checklist

  • I can explain the main rule in my own words.
  • I can identify the target form in a sentence.
  • I can explain why a common incorrect example is wrong.
  • I can apply the rule in a new sentence.

Do not judge mastery only by a score. If an answer was wrong, identify the rule behind the mistake and practise that pattern again.
